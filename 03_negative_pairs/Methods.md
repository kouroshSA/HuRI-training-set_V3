# Methods — In-Distribution Negative Pair Construction

## Purpose

`huri_interactome_uniprotID_seqs_cleaned_deduped_standard.csv` (50,176
positive interacting pairs, 8,008 unique proteins, from
`../01_cleaning_dedup_standardization/`) needs a matched set of negative
(non-interacting) pairs to serve as an in-distribution random control —
drawn from the *same* protein universe, so that a model trained or
evaluated on this data cannot separate positives from negatives just by
recognizing which proteins were "in the experiment" at all. It must instead
learn to recognize the interacting relationship itself.

Two requirements make this harder than uniform random pairing:

1. **No homology confound.** If a negative pair `(X, Y)` were built by
   swapping out `B` for `C` in a true pair `A–B`, but `C` happens to be a
   close paralog of `B`, then `A–C` is a bad negative: paralogs frequently
   share interaction partners, so `A` may well genuinely interact with `C`,
   and even where it doesn't, the model isn't being asked to distinguish
   interaction from non-interaction — it's being asked to distinguish `B`
   from a near-copy of `B`, a much easier and different task. This has to be
   checked against *every* true partner of `A`, not just the one partner `B`
   being replaced.
2. **Fair use of every protein.** Negatives should draw on each protein
   about as often as the positive set does, so that a protein's sheer
   frequency of appearance isn't itself a shortcut signal separating
   positives from negatives.

## Rejection rules

A candidate pair `(X, Y)` is only accepted as a negative if **all** of the
following hold, where `partners(P)` is the set of `P`'s true interacting
partners in the positive table, and "detectably similar" means the pair
appears at all in `protein_homology_table.csv` (built with mmseqs2 at
maximum sensitivity and a permissive E-value cutoff — see
`../02_homology_table/Methods.md` — so absence from that table means no
similarity was found even under a lenient search):

| Rule | Rejects if | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| R1 | `(X,Y)` (either orientation) is a true positive pair | negative pairs cannot themselves be interactions |
| R2 | `(X,Y)` (either orientation) was already chosen as a negative | no duplicate/swapped-orientation negatives |
| R3 | `X` and `Y` are themselves detectably similar to each other | a pair of near-paralogs is a poor "random" control regardless of who their partners are |
| R4 | `Y` is detectably similar to **any** protein in `partners(X)` | direct implementation of the requested check ("C of A–C must not resemble B of A–B"), generalized to all of `X`'s true partners, not only one |
| R5 | `X` is detectably similar to **any** protein in `partners(Y)` | the same check in the other direction |
| — | `X == Y` | self-pairs are excluded from this negative set entirely (see "Self-interactions" below) |

No numeric identity threshold was applied on top of this — presence in the
homology table (i.e. any detected similarity at all, down to the 14.2%
identity floor mmseqs2 reported under its permissive search settings) is
treated as disqualifying. This is the most stringent version of the rule
that the data supports, per the request to be "as stringent as possible."

## Target size and degree-preserving sampling

The number of negative pairs was fixed at exactly 50,176 — the same as the
positive set. To also "maximize use of the proteins" (rather than a plain
uniform random pairing, which would use hub proteins no more than obscure
ones), pairs were generated by a **degree-preserving stub/configuration-model
matching**:

1. Build a "stub list": each protein appears once for every positive pair it
   participates in (twice for a homodimer/self-interacting protein, the
   standard graph convention for a self-loop's contribution to degree). This
   list has exactly 100,352 entries — twice the pair count — so a perfect,
   collision-free matching of it yields exactly 50,176 pairs.
2. **Phase 1:** repeatedly shuffle the stub list and pair up consecutive
   entries; any pair that fails a rule above (or pairs a protein with
   itself) is set aside and re-shuffled into the next round. This converges
   quickly for the vast majority of proteins because most have few enough
   constraints that a compatible partner turns up within a handful of
   rounds.
3. **Phase 2 (top-up):** phase 1 can stall for a small number of high-degree
   or heavily-paralogous proteins, *not* because they lack valid partners
   overall, but because phase 1 only re-pairs a stuck protein against other
   still-unmatched proteins, and that shrinking pool can itself become
   mutually incompatible (e.g. several stuck hub proteins that all happen to
   be paralogs of each other). Phase 2 resolves every remaining stub against
   the **full** 8,008-protein pool: it first tries other still-needy
   proteins (to keep exact degree-matching as tight as possible), and only
   falls back to pairing with an already-satisfied protein (accepting a
   small, logged "overshoot" of +1 to that protein's negative-pair count) if
   no needy partner works.

### Self-interactions

The 470 positive self-interactions (`Protein_ID_1 == Protein_ID_2`,
homodimers) are not given negative counterparts — there is no meaningful
"random" self-pair to construct. Their stub contribution (each contributes 2
stubs, since a self-loop counts twice toward a node's degree) still has to
be absorbed into ordinary pairs with *other* proteins during construction,
which is handled automatically by the stub-matching process above.

## Results

- **50,176 / 50,176 negative pairs constructed** (exact target reached).
- Phase 1 alone reached 48,101 pairs (95.9%) over 311 rounds; phase 2's
  top-up closed the remaining 2,075 pairs, of which 2,048 required a
  full-pool overshoot partner (i.e. only 27 were resolved via another needy
  protein).
- **Degree-matching:** 6,037 / 8,008 proteins (75.4%) ended up used in
  exactly as many negative pairs as positive pairs. 246 proteins (3.1%) were
  under their target, concentrated in a handful of very high-degree or
  heavily-paralogous proteins that ran out of homology-safe candidates
  within the exact-degree accounting (the largest deficit, -153, belongs to
  the single highest-degree protein in the set, positive degree 484). 1,725
  proteins (21.5%) absorbed a small surplus (usually +1, up to +4) to
  compensate. The deficits and surpluses balance exactly (they must, since
  total pair count is fixed), and the surplus is spread thinly across a
  large number of proteins rather than concentrated.
- No positive-pair, duplicate, self-pair, or homology-rule violation
  survived: an **independent** re-verification (re-deriving the positive
  set, partner lists, and homology adjacency from scratch, not reusing the
  generator script's in-memory state) confirmed **zero violations** of any
  rule across all 50,176 output rows, zero duplicate/swapped-orientation
  rows, zero unknown protein IDs, and zero sequence mismatches against the
  canonical per-ID sequence lookup.

## On the "remove high-identity pairs instead" fallback

Removing highly-similar positive pairs to make negative construction easier
was considered and explicitly not needed: the degree-preserving construction
reached the exact target count (50,176) without weakening the homology rule
or touching the positive table at all. That fallback remains available if a
future, larger, or differently-thresholded homology table makes exact
degree-matching infeasible, but was not required here.
